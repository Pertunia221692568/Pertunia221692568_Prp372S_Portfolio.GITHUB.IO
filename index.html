Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

At the start of the project we were four students with different strengths, schedules and communication styles. Without a shared agreement we risked unequal participation, missed deadlines and scope creep.

Task

Create and live by a Team Charter that would serve as our north star — defining purpose, roles, working agreements, decision-making and a clear Definition of Done.

Action

We co-created the charter. I contributed to the Ways of Working section (Kanban columns and WIP limits of 2 cards per person), communication norms, and the risk register. We treated the document as a living artefact and revisited it after every retrospective.

Result

The charter significantly reduced friction. Stand-ups stayed focused, blockers were raised within 24 hours, and we achieved clear participation evidence on Trello. It turned a group of individuals into a functioning Agile team.

Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

After defining the product vision and core features, the team needed a clear technical structure so that development work remained aligned and feasible within the project timeline.

Task

Produce a solution architecture that illustrated the main components of LaunchPad.dev, the relationships between them, and how the system would support the four core features.

Action

I contributed to the design of the architecture diagram and the supporting document. We mapped the frontend, backend services, data storage, and external integrations, ensuring the design stayed realistic for a student WIL prototype while still demonstrating professional thinking.

03

User Stories & Tasks

Evidence

User stories written from the ICT student’s perspective and broken into small, estimable tasks with clear acceptance criteria. Managed on our Trello board.
Open Trello Board

Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

Early tasks were still high-level (“build the portfolio feature”), making it difficult to know when work was truly finished and causing uneven workload distribution.

Task

Break every feature into INVEST-style user stories, attach clear acceptance criteria, and decompose them into small tasks that could be completed within a few days.

Action

I participated in story-writing sessions, helped define acceptance criteria for each story, created and refined related tasks, and ensured every card had an owner, due date and checklist.

Result

By Sprint 3 the board was transparent. Everyone knew exactly what “done” looked like. Velocity improved and last-minute surprises before demos were significantly reduced.

Product Backlog

Evidence

Prioritised, living list of all work required to deliver LaunchPad.dev — features, technical tasks, research, documentation and final presentation items. Continuously refined with research findings and lecturer feedback.
Open Trello Board

Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

The early backlog was a simple list of ideas. As research findings and lecturer feedback arrived, new items appeared and priorities shifted, particularly around visual design.

Task

Maintain a prioritised Product Backlog that reflected current value, technical risk and learning, while remaining transparent to the whole team and the lecturer.

Action

I regularly refined the backlog with the team: prioritising items, splitting large work, adding research-driven stories, and using the Definition of Done as the filter for completion. Screenshots and a video walkthrough captured the evolution.

Result

The backlog stayed realistic and manageable. When final deployment and the pitch approached, the top of the backlog clearly showed what still needed to be finished, enabling focused and calm sprints.

05

Sprint Backlog

Evidence

This is part of the Trello Board for short sprints (usually four days) aligned with the academic calendar.
Open Trello Board

Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

With four team members juggling multiple subjects, long planning sessions were impractical. We needed short, focused sprints that still delivered visible progress every week.

Task

Create and manage Sprint Backlogs that were realistic for our capacity, clearly owned, and visible on Trello so progress and blockers were obvious at every stand-up.

Action

During Sprint Planning I helped select the highest-priority items that fitted our WIP limits, broke work into finishable cards, assigned owners and added checklists. Mid-sprint I updated cards daily and flagged risks early.

Result

We consistently moved cards to Done. The visual nature of the Sprint Backlogs made it easy to celebrate progress and re-plan when design changes were requested. By the final sprints we had a steady rhythm and a deployed application.

Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

Agile ceremonies only create lasting value if the conversations are captured and acted upon. Without a consistent logbook, insights from stand-ups and retrospectives would disappear after each meeting.

Task

Maintain an honest and consistent Agile Logbook that recorded daily progress, impediments and learning so that patterns became visible over the full project period.

Action

I treated the logbook as a non-negotiable habit. After every stand-up I recorded what I had completed, what I planned next, and any blockers (including personal ones such as competing subject deadlines). When the lecturer requested design improvements I documented the feedback, the actions taken, and the outcome.

Result

The logbook became clear evidence of continuous improvement. Looking back, the progression from early time-management challenges to consistent early risk-raising is visible. Writing weekly reflections also strengthened my ability to think critically about my own process.
