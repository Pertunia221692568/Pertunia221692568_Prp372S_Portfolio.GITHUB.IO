🌟 Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

At the start we were four students with different strengths and schedules. Without a shared agreement we risked unequal participation, missed deadlines and scope creep.

Task

Help create and live by a Team Charter that would serve as our north star — defining purpose, roles, working agreements, decision-making and a clear Definition of Done.

Action

We co-created the charter. I contributed to Ways of Working (Kanban columns + WIP limits of 2), communication norms and the risk register. We treated it as a living document and revisited it after every retrospective.

Result

Friction dropped dramatically. Stand-ups stayed focused, blockers were raised within 24 hours, and we achieved 100% participation evidence on Trello. The charter turned individuals into a real Agile team.

📁 Open full evidence folder

🌟 Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

Early tasks were still high-level (“build the portfolio feature”), making it hard to know when something was truly finished and causing uneven workload.

Task

Break every feature into INVEST-style user stories, attach acceptance criteria, and decompose them into small tasks that could be completed in a few days.

Action

I actively participated in story-writing workshops, helped define acceptance criteria, created/refined related tasks and made sure every card had an owner, due date and checklist.

Result

By Sprint 3 the board was transparent. Everyone knew exactly what “done” looked like. Velocity improved and last-minute surprises before demos disappeared.

Product Backlog

🖼️ Evidence

A prioritised, living list of everything needed to deliver LaunchPad.dev — features, technical work, research, documentation and final pitch tasks. Continuously refined with research findings and lecturer feedback.

📁 Open full evidence folder

🌟 Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

The early backlog was a simple list of ideas. As research and lecturer feedback arrived, new items appeared and priorities shifted (especially visual design).

Task

Maintain a prioritised Product Backlog that reflected current value, technical risk and learning, while keeping it transparent to the team and lecturer.

Action

I regularly refined the backlog with the team: prioritising, splitting large items, adding research-driven stories and using the Definition of Done as the exit filter. Screenshots and a video walkthrough captured the evolution.

Result

The backlog stayed healthy and realistic. When the final deployment and pitch approached, the top of the backlog clearly showed exactly what still needed finishing — giving us calm, focused sprints.

Sprint Backlog

🖼️ Evidence

Real-time, actionable plans pulled from the Product Backlog for short (usually 4-day) sprints aligned with our academic calendar. Examples: Sprint 1 focused on repository setup, Spring Boot, Figma design system and presentation updates.

📁 Open full evidence folder

🌟 Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

With four team members juggling multiple subjects, long planning sessions didn’t work. We needed short, focused sprints that still delivered visible progress every week.

Task

Create and manage Sprint Backlogs that were realistic for our capacity, clearly owned, and visible on Trello so progress and blockers were obvious at every stand-up.

Action

During Sprint Planning I helped select highest-priority items that fit our WIP limits, broke work into finishable cards, assigned owners and added checklists. Mid-sprint I updated cards daily and flagged risks early.

Result

We consistently moved cards to Done. The visual Sprint Backlogs made it easy to celebrate wins and re-plan when design changes were requested. By the final sprints we had a smooth rhythm and a deployed application.

Agile Logbook

🖼️ Evidence

Recurring themes: prototyping & refining wireframes after feedback, creating the GitHub repository, design system & colour scheme improvements, presentation prep, honest time-management challenges.

📁 Open all logbook entries (.docx)

🌟 Reflection — STAR Method

Situation

Agile ceremonies only create value if conversations are captured and acted upon. Without a logbook, insights from stand-ups and retrospectives would disappear after the meeting.

Task

Keep a consistent, honest Agile Logbook that recorded daily progress, impediments and learning so that patterns became visible over the months.

Action

I treated the logbook as a non-negotiable habit. After every stand-up I wrote what I completed, planned next, and any blockers (including personal ones). When the lecturer requested a more appealing colour scheme I recorded the feedback, redesign actions and outcome.

Result

The logbook became powerful evidence of continuous improvement. I can now see the journey from “time management is hard” to “we flag risks 48 hours early”. Writing weekly reflections trained me to think critically about my own process.

Building LaunchPad.dev under real Agile practices taught me far more than any textbook could.
